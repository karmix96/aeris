S8 · O-H structured · mesh method

Building the O-H Mesh

Seven steps from one aerofoil section to 603,592 cells. The part that trips everyone up is the last face: how a stack of closed O-rings gets capped by a single H patch without leaving a hole or a collapsed edge. Step 05 answers exactly that.

00the map, before the steps

Three blocks, and what each one is for

The domain is closed by three structured blocks. Two of them are the same annulus at different spanwise stations; the third is the odd one out, and it is the one worth understanding.

o_wing 93 × 65 × 49 · the wing itself o_out 93 × 65 × 54 · the annulus, continued cap_out 43 × 5 × 54 · the tip cap, continued

Every block uses the same three index directions, and the names are worth fixing now because the rest of the page leans on them:

the name

O is the topology in the section plane: one closed ring around the aerofoil, no branch cut, no wake block. H is the topology on the tip face: a single quad patch with four sides. It is O everywhere except the one face where it is H.

01one closed ring on one section

The ring is the whole surface mesh

Start with one aerofoil section off the exact pyGeo loft, after the trailing edge has been opened to 0.1 % of chord. Walk it once: upper trailing-edge corner → leading edge → lower trailing-edge corner → straight across the blunt base, and you are back where you started.

That last leg matters. The blunt base is part of the ring, not a separate block, which is why there is no base patch and no wake block anywhere in this mesh.

# section_o.py · section_ring()
up_targets = vinokur(n_side, ds_te, ds_le, up_len)   # TE corner -> LE
lo_targets = vinokur(n_side, ds_te, ds_le, lo_len)

ring = [up, lo[::-1][1:]]              # upper, then lower reversed
if n_base > 2:                             # then straight across the base
    t = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, n_base)[1:-1]
    ring.append(lo[0] + t[:, None] * (up[0] - lo[0]))
ring = np.vstack(ring)                    # 92 points, closed

Nodes are placed by vinokur(), two-sided hyperbolic stretching that hits a requested first and last interval exactly. It is solved by bisection on the tanh parameter, not by Vinokur's published asymptotic fits — those are accurate over only part of the range, and the one number this whole strategy exists to control is the spacing at the leading edge.

02the nose spacing is solved, not predicted

Ten degrees per cell, measured

Every other parameter on the ring is a number you type. The leading-edge spacing is not: it is solved for, so that each cell across the nose absorbs no more than a target amount of surface turning — 10° here.

The obvious shortcut is ds = R · θ from a circle fit across the nose. It does not work. On a real aerofoil the curvature is still changing fast over the first cell, and a three-point fit overestimates the nose radius by a factor of three: asking for 6° that way delivered 20°.

So the estimate comes out of the loop entirely. Build the ring, measure the turning on the ring you actually built, bisect on ds_le.

# section_o.py · ring_for_target_turning()
lo, hi = floor, 0.25 * le_arc
for _ in range(max_iterations):
    mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    ring, metrics = section_ring(..., ds_le=mid, ds_te=ds_te)
    got = metrics["le_turn_per_cell_deg"]   # measured, not modelled
    if abs(got - target_turn_deg) <= tolerance: break
    if got > target_turn_deg: hi = mid        # too coarse -> tighten
    else:                    lo = mid

section_o.py · 60 iterations, tolerance 0.02°

why this is the point of the strategy

Because the turning is delivered rather than hoped for, the same target produces a correctly resolved nose on every aerofoil in the design space — a 100-design LHS, not one hand-tuned wing. The earlier C family placed nodes by end_points and could not give that guarantee.

03stack the rings

Forty-nine rings make a surface

Repeat step 02 at 49 spanwise stations. Same ring topology every time, same 92 nodes, different chord and different solved nose spacing. Stack them along ζ and the surface mesh is finished — node i on every station is the same point on the aerofoil, so the surface is a clean structured sheet with no interpolation anywhere.

One guard on the stack

Because the nose spacing is solved independently at each station, neighbouring stations can disagree. Left alone, the worst station-to-station jump in leading-edge spacing was 2.52×; that is a kink in the surface mesh. A growth limit of 1.15× is applied along the span, which clamped 13 of 49 stations and reduced one station's spacing by at most 2.65×.

04march each ring to the far field

From the wall out to a circle

Each ring is now marched outward along η through 65 layers, from a first cell of 4.71 µm to a far-field circle 34.55 m away — seven orders of magnitude, on a geometric distribution growing 1.253× per layer.

This is an algebraic march, deliberately. pyHyp segfaulted 25 times on the earlier D family; a cubic Hermite version of this march folded about 50 mm out, on layers 35–51 of 65. What survives is simple: at every layer, blend the smoothed wall-normal direction toward the purely radial one, then relax.

# march_o.py · march_section(), inside the layer loop
normals = _outward_normals(current, normal_smoothing)
radial  = (current - centre2); radial /= norm(radial)

w = float(eta[k]) ** radial_blend_power    # LAYER fraction, not distance
direction = (1.0 - w) * normals + w * radial
direction /= norm(direction)               # wall-normal -> radial

march_o.py · normals recomputed on the layer just built, every layer

Two details in those four lines were each a bug. The blend runs on the layer fraction, not the distance fraction: with geometric growth the distance fraction is under 1 % until the last three layers, so any blend keyed to distance does nothing at all and then everything at once, exactly where cells are kilometres wide. And the Laplacian relaxation smooths the step, never the coordinates — relaxing absolute positions drags the first layers along the wall and drove median wall orthogonality error from 0.2° to 75°.

05close the tip

How the ring becomes an H patch

Here is the step that is hard to picture. After step 04 the wing has a surface and a volume around it, but the tip face is still open: inside the tip ring there is a hole shaped like an aerofoil, and it has to be filled with quads.

The trick is a counting coincidence that is not a coincidence at all, because it was designed: cut the 92-node ring into four pieces, and those four pieces are exactly the four sides of a 43 × 5 rectangle.

# strategy_s8.py · build_tip_cap()
half_nose = (n_base - 1) // 2          # n_base must be ODD: 5 -> 2

nose       = np.vstack([upper[le-half_nose:], lower[1:half_nose+1]])  # 5
upper_side = upper[: le - half_nose + 1][::-1]                      # 43
lower_side = lower[half_nose:]                                     # 43
base       = np.vstack([lower_side[-1:], base_interior, upper_side[-1:]]) # 5

assert len(nose) == len(base) == n_base
assert len(upper_side) == len(lower_side) == n_chord

strategy_s8.py · then transfinite interpolation on those four sides, then 400 Laplacian sweeps

Read the counts again: nose = 5 and base = 5. The nose arc carries the same number of points as the blunt base. That single equality is what lets one quad patch close the loop, and it is why n_base is required to be odd — so the nose arc can be centred on the leading-edge node with half_nose on each side.

What happens if you do it any other way

the butterfly, measured

The first version did pull the ring inward toward its centroid and fill the middle with a transfinite square. On a circle that is textbook. On an aerofoil it is nonsense, and measurably so: the tip ring's distance to its centroid ranges 7.7 mm to 80.2 mm, a ratio of 10.4, so a 65 % pull moves points up to 52 mm across a section only 22.9 mm thick. Upper and lower surfaces crossed. The collar reached a minimum scaled Jacobian of 0.0075 with a 180° normal flip, and the centre patch went negative at −0.1485.

Chords across the thickness cannot cross, because the section is convex in that direction everywhere. That is the geometric fact the whole construction rests on.

Now put the ring and the patch in one picture

This is the answer to “how do they combine?”. Look at the tip plane face-on. The H patch fills the inside of the ring. The O annulus grows from the outside of the same ring. They share the ring itself, node for node.

06sweep it outboard

The easiest step in the build

Everything outboard of the tip is a pure translation of that disc along +y. Two lines:

# build_volume.py
offsets, span_growth = march_o.geometric_distribution(n_out, first_out, radius)
shift = np.zeros((n_out, 3)); shift[:, 1] = offsets

blocks["o_out"]   = volume[:, :, -1][:, :, None, :] + shift[None, None, :, :]
blocks["cap_out"] = cap_patch[:, :, None, :]      + shift[None, None, :, :]

build_volume.py · n_out chosen from a growth ceiling of 1.30, not from the span count

One number that is not arbitrary

The tip cap is a viscous wall whose normal is the spanwise direction, so the first outboard cell sets y+ on the cap. Using the wing's own tip spanwise cell of 0.95 mm would give the cap a first cell 200× the wing's 4.7 µm, and a y+ to match. Instead the spanwise law is asked to deliver a tip cell of 2.0 × s₀, so the cap is wall-resolved and the o_wing/o_out interface carries no cell-size jump at all.

The measured effect of getting this right: cap y+ went 4.16 → 1.46 → 1.27 across the fix.

07verify, then accept

Nothing here is accepted on the strength of looking right

A transfinite patch will happily interpolate a perimeter assembled in the wrong order and hand back a folded result that still looks plausible in ParaView. Reversing the base once drove the patch's minimum scaled Jacobian to −0.977. So the perimeter is checked against the ring rather than assumed to match it:

perimeter = np.vstack([
    patch[0, :, :],        # nose arc, lower -> upper
    patch[1:, -1, :],      # upper surface, nose -> TE
    patch[-1, -2::-1, :],  # base, upper -> lower
    patch[-2::-1, 0, :],   # lower surface, TE -> nose
])
gap = np.abs(np.sort(perimeter[:-1], axis=0) - np.sort(ring, axis=0)).max()
if gap > 1.0e-12:
    raise AssertionError(f"patch perimeter is not the tip ring: {gap:.3e} m apart")

The fold ladder

Cell volumes are computed by decomposition into six tetrahedra, not by the mean-Jacobian shortcut — the shortcut agrees on well-shaped cells and hides the twisted ones, which are exactly the cells worth finding. If any hex is inverted, the normal smoothing is raised and every section is re-marched:

SMOOTHING_LADDER = (4, 8, 15, 25, 40, 60)

The ladder climbs on the assembled 3-D hexes, not on in-plane quads. That distinction cost a bug: the in-plane test cannot see a fold that lives between two neighbouring sections, and on one geometry it was clean on every section while the assembled volume carried 645 folds. The ladder had stopped at its first rung with an invalid grid.

what refuses to build

A mesh with folded cells, a tip cap whose first cell exceeds 3 × s₀, an even n_base, a perimeter that is not the ring, or a wall layer that has left the loft by more than 1e−9 m. Each of those raises rather than warns. Overrides exist (--allow-folded-cells, --allow-coarse-tip-cap) and each one has to be typed.
