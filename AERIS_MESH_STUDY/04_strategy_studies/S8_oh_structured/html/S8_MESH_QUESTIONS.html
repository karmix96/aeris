S8 · companion to “Building the O-H Mesh”

Six Questions About the Mesh

Straight answers, measured where they can be measured. Updated 24 September, with twelve solves in. Q3 originally blamed the coarse wake for the unexplained grid sensitivity. That was refuted for free from meshes already on disk. The real lever is chordwise resolution — now confirmed on three geometries — and the far-field domain has been tested on forces for the first time. Results at the bottom.

01is the tip closure novel?

No. It is a competent construction, not a contribution

not novel

Two things I found that say so directly:

  • NASA's WINGCAP, part of Chimera Grid Tools, has generated wing-tip cap grids for years with a documented choice of O-, C- or H-topology for the cap, plus a special H-cap for cusped sections. Capping a tip with an H-patch is a menu option in a production tool.
  • O-H for a blunt-trailing-edge aerofoil is published: the O wraps the section and its boundary layer as a closed loop precisely so there is no mesh discontinuity at the trailing edge. That is our topology, described by someone else.

What is specific to S8 is narrower than a topology. It is a counting constraint: make the nose arc carry the same node count as the blunt base, force n_base odd, and one transfinite patch closes the ring with zero collapsed edges and zero interpolation. I did not find that stated as a named result — but I also could not open the one paper most likely to contain it (MDPI returned 403), so “I did not find it” is weak evidence, not a novelty claim.

do not put this in an abstract

If S8 has a defensible contribution it is not here. It is (a) the curvature-driven leading-edge spacing that stays systematically refinable — scaling the turning target alone refined delivered spacing by only 1.142 when asked for 1.30, and fixing that is what makes the grid-convergence family mean anything — and (b) verification-first automation. The tip cap belongs in a methods section, in one paragraph.

02how does refinement happen?

One ratio, every direction, 1.300

measured and clean

A convergence family has to be the same mesh at a different spacing, not three meshes that happen to differ in cell count. So one number, r = 1.3, scales every interval count and every absolute spacing request together:

What that looks like

the trap that was in here

The leading-edge target is a turning angle and the trailing-edge spacing is a chord fraction. Both are absolute constraints, so holding them fixed across levels looks like a safeguard and is the opposite: it freezes the cell size at the two places that matter most. Measured on the first attempt, trailing-edge spacing refined at 1.000 exactly across four levels and the leading edge went 0.881, 0.963, 1.009 — slightly coarser — while s0 refined at 1.30.

03what must the mesh capture?

It does the wing. It does not do the wake

body yes, wake no — but the wake is not the problem

At Ma 0.0837 and Re 1.53e6 the flow is easy in every way that usually hurts: incompressible, attached at these incidences, no shocks, no shock-boundary-layer interaction, fully turbulent by assumption. That leaves a short list of things the mesh actually has to resolve.

I proposed this as the cause of the GCI band. It is not.

The argument was reasonable: a half-wing at AR 4.836 carries a lot of induced drag, induced drag reaches the force integral through downwash set by trailing vorticity, and that vorticity is being convected through cells 2.9 per chord wide. Grid-dependent diffusion there would improve on every refinement — the signature of the unexplained 22–35 % GCI band.

It took no compute at all to kill. Six mesh variants were already built and solved. Measuring wake resolution on each and lining it up against the drag they actually produced:

What the lever actually is

Chordwise resolution, and it shows up entirely in pressure drag. Refining chordwise alone recovers more of the coarse-to-fine offset than refining everything at once does, at fewer cells.

why this is good news

The chordwise-only ladder reaches gci_F's chordwise resolution at 975k cells instead of 2.32M, and it is better conditioned. If it holds up, the cloud batch can be re-scoped around chord-only ladders rather than global ones.

04why are the extrusion lines curved?

They are not, past the first millimetre

and they must be

The marching line has two incompatible jobs. At the wall it must leave perpendicular to the surface, or the boundary-layer cells are sheared and the skin friction is wrong. At the far field it must be radial, so the outer boundary is a clean circle and neighbouring spanwise stations cannot cross. Those are different directions, so the line has to turn from one to the other. That turn is the curvature you are seeing.

But look at where the turn actually is:

Could we make them genuinely straight?

Yes, and it would ruin the mesh. Straight lines radiating from one centre is the simplest possible construction. Here is what it does at the wall:

the answer in one line

The lines curve because orthogonality at the wall is worth more than straightness, and the cost of buying it is 0.045 % extra path length. That is one of the cheapest trades in the whole mesh.

05the sweep is an extrusion — why no intersection?

Because it is a translation, and translations copy

0.0 m, exactly

A general march moves each node in its own direction, so neighbouring columns can close on each other and invert — that is the whole reason march_o.py needs a smoothing ladder and a fold check. The outboard sweep is not a march. Every node moves by the same vector.

claimmeasured on gci_C

That last row is the interesting one, and it does not break anything. The tip plane is not a flat constant-y slice: the span_normal frame lets every node keep its own out-of-plane offset, so the tip ring wanders 2.12 mm in y. It does not matter, because a rigid translation preserves whatever shape it is handed. The block is a sheared stack of copies of a valid tiling, and a copy cannot intersect itself.

The other half of “does not intersect” is that the tip plane is tiled before it is swept: the H patch covers the inside of the ring, the O annulus covers the outside, and they share the ring node-for-node. No overlap, no hole — so sweeping produces no overlap and no hole.

—validating the method further

BlendedNet++ can verify the pipeline. It cannot validate the drag

BlendedNet++ is a 12,490-case RANS dataset of blended wing bodies with integrated forces and dense surface C_p / C_f fields. It is a better geometric match to S8 than anything in the current validation plan — S8's own geometry is a BWB half wing, and RIBES is a conventional tapered wing. I checked how usable it actually is.

I pulled the case index out of the archive to check the overlap concretely. Of 9,992 training cases, 169 sit within ±0.02 in Mach of S8's point, within 3× in Reynolds, and inside our incidence range. 24 of those are in the first 1.9 GB of the 26 GB split archive, so a working subset costs one file, not the whole dataset.

the one number that decides how to use it

Their own stated mesh-independence error on C_D is under 8 %. At the matching subset's median C_D of 227 counts, that is ±18 counts. S8 exists to resolve effects of 5–9 counts. Their drag is two to three times too uncertain to validate ours — and because both codes run Spalart–Allmaras, agreement would not be evidence about physics anyway. It would be evidence that two SA solvers agree.

So it is verification, not validation, and that distinction is the whole answer:

  • Use it for cross-code, cross-paradigm checking of the pipeline — surface C_p fields and C_L on the same geometry. Structured O-H + ADflow against unstructured + FUN3D would catch a reference-area error, a lift-index error, a bad tip cap, a units slip. Those are exactly the failures this project has actually had.
  • Use it for a capability test: can our O-H mesher handle their BWB centrebody at all? That is a question S8 needs answered regardless, and here it comes with an answer key.
  • Do not use it for drag increments, and do not call agreement validation.
  • It does not replace RIBES. An experiment at the right Reynolds number is still the only thing that can test whether the physics is right rather than merely consistent.
—twelve solves, 24 September

What the compute bought

All twelve converged and were accepted by the gate: residuals 6.3–7.8 orders, C_L and C_D settled to better than 0.02 % over the tail. Three questions, three answers.

1. Is 40 root chords far enough? — Yes, with a stated bias

This study had never tested domain independence on forces. It now has. The 40-chord domain carries a consistent +0.65 count bias against 60 chords, the same sign and size at both incidences — systematic, not noise. Against a 5–9 count target effect that is 7–13 %: small enough not to block anything, large enough that it belongs in the uncertainty budget rather than being rounded away.

2. Does the chordwise order hold? — Partly, and that is the honest answer

So: the chordwise family is genuinely better conditioned than the global one (GCI21 11.0–12.9 % on C_D against 22–35 %) and it is converging at roughly second order. It is not in a tight asymptotic range. The extrapolation carries about six counts of its own uncertainty, which is the size of the thing we are trying to measure. 75 chordwise points is not enough, and this is exactly what gci_C_chord3 at 98 points was built to settle.

3. Does chordwise dominance generalise? — Yes, clearly

Six independent cases, three different wings, each with its own reference area. This is a property of the mesh strategy, not of geometry 83.

the finding that changes the cloud invoice

gci_F — the level the cloud batch was scoped around — has n_side 75. So does gci_C_chord2, which runs on the desktop. They are chordwise-identical. gci_F costs 2,322,128 cells against 974,800, and every one of that extra 1.35M buys refinement in the two directions just measured not to carry the error.

gci_C_chord3 at 98 points has more chordwise resolution than gci_F for 1,367,416 cells — 59 % of the cost. It is built, clean and on disk. At tonight's measured rate (35.7 min per million cells on 6 ranks) the planned gci_F batch is about 55 hours of wall time for 10 geometries × 4 incidences; the same batch on chord3 is about 32 hours, with better chordwise resolution.

the caveat, stated plainly

A formal ASME V&V 20 grid-convergence index requires uniform refinement in all directions. A chord-only ladder is a directional sensitivity, not a legal GCI, so it cannot replace the global family for the uncertainty claim — it can only tell us where the error lives and how to spend the fine-grid budget. The right move is not "cancel gci_F"; it is "buy gci_C_chord3 first, because it is the only thing that can tell us whether p stabilises, and that is what currently blocks a defensible number."

Every number on this page was measured on the built meshes at geometry lhs100_seed42[83] — the refinement family from gci_CC to gci_FF, and the marching, sweep and wake geometry from gci_C. The novelty answer in Q1 is a literature read, not a measurement, and is the one answer here I would not defend without a proper search.
